# Asset Pricing and the Bid-Ask Spread

**Source:** Amihud & Mendelson (1986) · Journal of Financial Economics · DOI: 10.1016/0304-405X(86)90065-6

## TL;DR

Assets with larger bid-ask spreads earn higher gross expected returns — investors demand compensation for transaction costs. The relationship is **concave**, because spread costs are amortized over the holding period, producing a clientele effect: long-horizon investors hold high-spread (illiquid) assets. This is the foundational theory-and-evidence paper of the **liquidity premium**, later operationalized by Amihud (2002).

## What anomaly it documents

- **Predictor:** the relative bid-ask spread (a direct measure of round-trip transaction cost).
- **Direction:** positive — higher-spread assets have higher gross expected returns.
- **Shape — the key contribution:** the spread-return relation is **concave** (increasing but at a decreasing rate). Because per-period spread cost falls the longer you hold, investors with longer horizons rationally sort into higher-spread assets (the **clientele/horizon effect**), bidding their prices up and flattening the curve at the illiquid end.
- OSAP predictor: **BidAskSpread**.

## How to construct it

- **Sorting variable:** relative quoted bid-ask spread = (ask − bid) / midpoint.
- **Universe:** NYSE stocks in the original study (1961–1980).
- **Portfolio formation:** rank into spread groups (the paper uses spread-and-beta double sorts).
- **Long / short:** long high-spread (illiquid), short low-spread (liquid).
- **Weighting:** the empirical tests use portfolio groupings controlling for beta.
- **Rebalancing:** periodic (annual in spirit).

## Evidence and replication

| Period | Notes | Source |
|--------|-------|--------|
| IS (1961–1980) | significant positive, concave spread-return relation | this paper |
| OOS (post-1986) | liquidity premium persists; spreads have shrunk structurally | post-publication |
| OSAP replication (BidAskSpread) | clear, positive | Chen & Zimmermann 2022 |

- The paper provides both an **equilibrium model** (the spread is amortized over the holding period; in equilibrium higher-spread assets must offer higher gross returns) and supporting cross-sectional evidence.
- It is the conceptual parent of the entire liquidity-premium literature: Amihud (2002) ILLIQ, Pástor-Stambaugh (2003) liquidity *risk*, and Acharya-Pedersen (2005) liquidity-adjusted CAPM all build on it.
- Like other liquidity effects, much of the cross-sectional premium overlaps with size and has compressed as market structure (decimalization, electronic trading) cut spreads dramatically.

## Why it might work

- **Transaction-cost compensation (the model):** investors require higher gross returns to hold assets that are costly to trade; in equilibrium net-of-cost returns equalize across the clientele structure.
- **Clientele / horizon sorting:** the concavity arises because patient, long-horizon investors can amortize spreads and thus accept lower gross returns on illiquid assets than impatient investors would — an elegant, testable prediction.
- **Liquidity as priced risk:** later work reframes part of the premium as compensation for *systematic* liquidity risk, not just the level of trading cost.

## Limitations and risks

- **Self-defeating costs:** the premium exists precisely because the assets are expensive to trade — capturing it means paying the spreads it compensates, so net alpha is small for active traders and best earned by buy-and-hold clienteles.
- **Structural spread decline:** spreads are far tighter today than in the 1961–1980 sample, shrinking the available premium.
- **Size entanglement:** spread, size, and illiquidity are tightly correlated; the standalone spread premium is modest after controls.
