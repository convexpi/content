# Illiquidity and Stock Returns: Cross-Section and Time-Series Effects

**Source:** Amihud (2002) · Journal of Financial Markets · DOI: 10.1016/S1386-4181(01)00024-6 · [Full text (PDF)](http://archive.nyu.edu/bitstream/2451/26706/2/FIN-00-041.pdf)

## TL;DR

Less-liquid stocks earn higher average returns: the **illiquidity premium**. Amihud introduces a simple, data-cheap illiquidity measure (ILLIQ = average daily |return| per dollar of volume) and shows that (i) in the cross-section, illiquid stocks command higher expected returns, and (ii) in the time series, expected market illiquidity positively predicts market excess returns.

## What anomaly it documents

The paper operationalizes the Amihud-Mendelson (1986) liquidity premium with a measure computable from daily return and volume data alone.

- **Predictor:** ILLIQ — the average daily absolute return scaled by dollar trading volume; a proxy for price impact (how much prices move per dollar traded).
- **Direction:** positive — higher illiquidity predicts higher expected return.
- **Two distinct results:** (1) *cross-sectional* — illiquid stocks earn a return premium; (2) *time-series* — when expected market illiquidity is high, subsequent market excess returns are high, while an unexpected illiquidity shock depresses prices contemporaneously ("flight to liquidity").
- OSAP predictor: **Illiquidity**.

## How to construct it

- **Sorting variable:** ILLIQ_i = (1/D_i) · Σ_d |R_{i,d}| / DVOL_{i,d}, where R is the daily return, DVOL is daily dollar volume, averaged over the days D in the year. Higher = more illiquid.
- **Universe:** NYSE common stocks in the original study (1964–1997); exclude stocks with too few valid trading days and extreme low-price names.
- **Portfolio formation:** annually, using prior-year ILLIQ.
- **Long / short:** long high-ILLIQ (illiquid), short low-ILLIQ (liquid).
- **Weighting:** equal-weighted (the effect concentrates in smaller names); control for size, beta, and momentum in cross-sectional regressions.
- **Rebalancing:** annual.

- ILLIQ has become one of the most-used liquidity proxies precisely because it requires only daily data, correlating well with finer microstructure measures.
- The cross-sectional premium is partly entangled with **size** (small stocks are mechanically illiquid), so controls matter.
- Out of sample the premium persists but is compressed as liquidity has broadly improved (decimalization, electronic markets).

## Why it might work

- **Liquidity premium (risk/clientele):** investors require compensation for higher trading costs and for the risk of needing to sell into an illiquid market (Amihud-Mendelson 1986). This is the leading, risk/frictions-based explanation.
- **Liquidity risk:** Pástor-Stambaugh (2003) and Acharya-Pedersen (2005) extend this to *systematic* liquidity risk — assets that become illiquid when the market does carry extra premium.
- **Flight to liquidity:** in stress, capital crowds into liquid assets, depressing illiquid prices and raising their forward returns.

## Limitations and risks

- **Self-defeating costs:** the premium compensates for trading costs, so capturing it requires trading exactly the stocks that are expensive to trade — much of the paper premium is eaten by real-world execution.
- **Size entanglement:** ILLIQ is strongly correlated with market cap; the standalone illiquidity premium net of size is smaller.
- **Capacity:** concentrated in small, illiquid names — very limited scalable capacity.
- **Measurement:** ILLIQ is noisy for low-volume names and sensitive to the day-count and outlier handling.
